While acute sleep deprivation is known to impair working memory, its long-term effect on emotional memory consolidation remains debated [14]. In this randomized crossover trial ($n = 412$ healthy adults, aged 19 to 38), participants were assigned to either a normal-sleep condition or 24 hours of supervised wakefulness following prose encoding. Delayed recall was assessed 72 hours later using standardized accuracy scoring, controlling for baseline anxiety and caffeine intake.

Rested participants recalled significantly more neutral details than sleep-deprived peers ($p < 0.001$), whereas retention of negatively valenced sentences differed only modestly between groups ($p = 0.04$). These findings suggest that brief sleep loss preferentially disrupts neutral contextual binding rather than erasing emotional salience altogether. However, because daytime microsleeps were not continuously monitored via scalp electroencephalography, we cannot rule out compensatory offline consolidation during recovery rest. Further longitudinal cohorts are needed to establish causality.
