Every genuine act of remembering involves a quiet paradox: the past is given to us only insofar as it is held within a present perspective that cannot itself become an object of detached inspection. If an empirical account tries to reduce recollection to stored traces alone, it mistakes a necessary biological condition for a sufficient explanation of lived experience.

Notice what this entails for self-knowledge. In one sense, I am never identical with the earlier self whose hesitation I recall with embarrassment; yet in another sense, that very distance furnishes the ground upon which moral continuity becomes intelligible at all. To ask whether memory merely mirrors a finished fact, or actively gathers what would otherwise slip into indifference, is already to recognize that knowing oneself is neither a private inventory nor a fixed possession, but an ongoing interpretation answerable to truth.
